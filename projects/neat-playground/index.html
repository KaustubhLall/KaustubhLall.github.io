NEAT neuroevolution implemented from scratch in C, evolving neural nets that play Flappy Bird, Snake, 2048, and Pac-Man, with interactive replay visualizers you can run in the browser.

A from-scratch C implementation of NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies) that evolves small neural networks to play four arcade games, using episode fitness without action labels or gradient descent. Recorded evolution histories replay in interactive browser visualizers showing the network, its sensors, and its decisions.

Overview

NEAT Playground trains neural networks through evolution. A population of small networks plays Flappy Bird, Snake, 2048, or Pac-Man, and selected performers reproduce with mutations to their weights and structure. Episode fitness guides the search without action labels or gradient descent. The method is NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies), built from scratch in C99, driving all four games through one shared interface. The repository reports throughput of about 60 million agent-steps per second per core; that figure was not re-benchmarked for this case study.

The repository began as a two-line README in April 2019. The retained implementation and four-game study were committed in July 2026, followed by browser replay work. Its central comparison asks whether changing the training worlds helps: train the same algorithm on one fixed world instead of a fresh random one every generation, and all four games score lower on an unseen world after fixed-world training than after random-world training in the recorded ablation.

The problem and motivation

I wanted to train agents from their performance in a game, without collecting examples of the correct action at each step. The simulator can score a completed episode, but that score does not explain which earlier decisions helped. This project uses neuroevolution to search directly over the controller and its network structure.

NEAT represents a network's weights and connections as a genome, evaluates its episode fitness, and uses selection and mutation to produce the next population. It requires neither action labels nor gradients through the simulator. Sparse or delayed rewards can still make useful candidates hard to discover: a fitness score must distinguish enough behaviors for selection to make progress. The sensor and fitness choices therefore remain part of the learning problem.

The four games form a difficulty ladder, not four unrelated demos: Flappy is a pure reflex baseline, Snake adds path planning, 2048 adds reading a whole board over many moves, and Pac-Man adds pursuing one goal while evading another. Holding the algorithm fixed and varying only the game turns each result into a question about what NEAT does under different demands.

How it evolved (2019 to 2026)

The April 2019 commit records the AiBirb name and the intention to use NEAT for Flappy Bird. The first retained implementation arrived in July 2026: a C simulator, shared-memory transport, a viewer, and a trainer. Subsequent commits moved game behavior behind the Env interface and added Snake, 2048, and Pac-Man. That interface let the same evolutionary machinery meet different observation spaces and action choices.

The study then made the training setup itself a subject of comparison: fixed versus changing worlds, fitness versus batch-based champion selection, and feedforward versus recurrent networks. Saved logs keep those questions inspectable after a run ends. The browser replay work provides another view of the recorded agents, while the CSV downloads below expose the measurements behind the learning curves and selection comparison.

System architecture

One NEAT engine drives all four games through a single shared interface, the Env vtable in include/env.h: observe, step, reset, alive, score. The engine is 2,086 lines across 7 files (genome operations, population and speciation, network compilation, HyperNEAT, config parsing, serialization, and the RNG), game-agnostic by design. Three driver binaries, a trainer, a viewer, and a server, all reach every game through that vtable, and each of the four game modules, 1,238 lines total, implements it independently: adding a fifth game means one new file behind the vtable, not touching the engine or the drivers. The whole tree is 4,698 lines across 21 files.

The evolutionary core is canonical NEAT: every new connection gets a global innovation number so differently-shaped networks can still be recombined correctly, and new structure is protected from immediate competition by speciation, grouping networks by a compatibility distance (excess genes, disjoint genes, average weight difference) and breeding mostly within each species using fitness sharing, with stagnant species removed while the top species and the all-time champion stay protected. A master seed drives separate game and evolution RNG streams. The default build avoids architecture-specific and fast-math flags, and the regression script compares the saved champions and logs from two same-seed runs of the same binary. Cross-platform byte equality has not been established. Champion-selection batches also derive their seeds from the master seed and generation number, making the selection inputs reproducible.

The browser visualizers load compressed JSON histories containing species networks and generation-specific evaluation-course seeds. A JavaScript port of the NEAT evaluator and all four environments executes the selected network and displays its activations and sensor readings. Keeping that second implementation consistent with C is a separate engineering task. The downloadable replay check below covers four diagnostic episodes; it does not certify every species and course in the histories.

The games and evaluation design

Each game hands the network a fixed-size sensor vector and gets back one action, chosen by argmax over the output layer. In the hosted replay histories, Flappy Bird uses 5 sensors and 2 actions, glide or flap. Snake uses 31 inputs: 7 heading-relative features plus 24 ray readings, with 3 relative-turn actions. 2048 combines its 16 board cells with 8 engineered features for 24 inputs and 4 slide actions. Pac-Man combines 16 features with 24 ray readings for 40 inputs and 4 move actions. These counts describe the hosted replay runs; the historical study results below retain their separate measurement provenance.

The default training setup derives a new batch of world seeds for each generation. Flappy's pipe gaps, Snake's start cell and apple positions, 2048's tile spawns, and Pac-Man's maze (a lattice of pillars, each staying a wall about 70% of the time, always generated from a fully-connected base so no maze is unsolvable) are drawn from the run's RNG stream. The memorization ablation below compares fixed-world and random-world training on unseen worlds. Random training worlds reduce the opportunity to memorize one level, but held-out evaluation is still needed to measure generalization.

Champion selection follows the same logic. Instead of crowning whichever genome had the highest fitness ever recorded, rewarding whoever's generation served up an easy world, the trainer periodically re-tests the top genomes on a brand-new random batch and crowns whoever scores best on it. A separate verifier can re-test the saved champion across a specified seed range, reporting mean, minimum, and maximum scores. Its printed mean is integer-truncated; the selection comparison below retains those printed batch means.

Experiments and results

The memorization ablation is the centerpiece: the one experiment that could have falsified the random-world design. Setup: train the same population two ways, once on a single world reused for the entire run ("fixed world") and once on a fresh random world every generation ("random world," the default), then score both on a world neither ever saw. A 2048 population trained on a fixed world has a reported score of 128 on that world and 6 on an unseen one. Trained on random worlds instead, it holds at 64 on training and 42 unseen. Snake shows the same shape (31 fixed-world training, 10 unseen, versus 12 and 25 trained random), as does Pac-Man (40 and 15, versus 66 and 41). Flappy shows the same advantage for random-world training: fixed-world training scores 6 on the training world and 2 unseen, while random-world training scores 54 on both. Snake's random-world unseen score (25) even edges past its own random-world training score (12), a reminder of how noisy one run is at this scale.

The learning curves average five runs at each generation. Their final logged test-batch means average 54 pipes for Flappy, 27.6 apples for Snake, and 32.8 pellets for Pac-Man. For 2048, the score is the largest tile reached in an episode, not the accumulated merge score. The trainer averages that value across its test batch and truncates it to an integer. At generation 299, the five logged values were 42, 38, 339, 40, and 54: a mean of 102.6 with a standard deviation of 118.3 across runs. That spread matters more than the mean alone. The late increase in the average curve does not establish a grokking transition or explain what the high-scoring run learned.

Two further three-seed ablations tested champion selection and recurrence. Selection on fresh test batches has slightly higher reported averages than selection on training fitness in three games: Flappy 54 versus 51, Snake 27 versus 26, and 2048 38 versus 35, with Pac-Man unchanged at 30, all rounded. These summaries do not establish statistical certainty. An early apparent recurrence advantage turned out to be confounded, as the later experiment explains.

A separate four-run, 800-generation 2048 study recorded peak logged test-batch mean maximum tiles of 46, 53, 65, and 47. The 65 is a batch average, not a tile value. That study used feedforward networks, a larger population, different observations, and longer test episodes than the five-run learning study. Its peaks and the learning study's final means are different measurements, so they cannot establish a gain from training longer.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Writing NEAT from scratch in C gave me control over both the evolutionary operators and the simulation cost. Small environments and batched population evaluation made repeated sweeps practical on commodity hardware. The cost is maintaining the machinery a mature library would already provide: mutation operators, serialization, visualization support, and established behavior to compare against when a run goes wrong.

Varying the training worlds is a deliberate choice: it reduces repeated exposure to one fixed layout. The ablation supports that choice for the measured runs, while separate unseen-world evaluation measures how much of the learned behavior transfers. A related choice was what the network should see. A raw board grid failed outright on the harder games (Snake scored 0 apples every time, Pac-Man about 7), in the evaluated configuration with 148 to 251 raw inputs. Those runs motivated the hand-built feature vectors used in the reported results, without establishing that NEAT cannot learn from raw boards under other configurations.

Keeping training in C and replay in JavaScript makes the saved networks easy to inspect in a browser, but leaves two implementations to maintain. A September consistency check rebuilt the native recorder and replayed four existing diagnostic cases on seed 12345, with no new training or holdout evaluation. Across 2,039 recorded steps, it compared observations, actions, scores, neuron activations and rendered state, then each final score. The networks occur in the public histories, but this diagnostic seed is not one of their recorded courses. The downloadable inputs and checker make that bounded comparison repeatable; final-state and termination checks remain outside the recorded evidence.

The newest work in the same tree raises the engineering bar again: C++20 and CMake instead of hand-rolled Makefiles, sanitizer builds, a ctest suite, and a held-out verifier as a first-class command rather than a line in a training log, aimed at a fifth game, a Frogger-style lane crossing that adds moving-obstacle timing to the planning and board-reading the first four games already demanded.

Lessons learned

Random-world training had higher unseen-world scores in all four recorded game comparisons. That supports keeping varied training environments in this project. The exported summaries do not establish a universal cause of memorization, matched evaluation horizons, or uncertainty across repeated runs. A replicated comparison with matched training and evaluation budgets would test how robust the observed advantage is.

The second lesson is a methodology warning that cost a real result to learn. An early one-factor-at-a-time sweep showed recurrent connections nearly doubling 2048's score, a headline-looking finding. Re-running the comparison from an already-tuned baseline instead of a default one made the effect mostly disappear: feedforward and recurrent networks landed within a couple of points of each other (45 versus 44). The apparent recurrence advantage depended on the comparison baseline. These runs did not establish an independent benefit after the other settings were tuned, so the earlier headline was too strong.

A third lesson: an easy benchmark tells you almost nothing about an algorithm, since it never exercises the parts that make the algorithm interesting. Flappy ignored nearly every hyperparameter that mattered for the other three games, so judged on Flappy alone, the honest conclusion would have been that none of NEAT's knobs matter, exactly wrong for the rest.

Current limitations

The repository is private, so this page has no code link. Selected ablation summaries, source identities, a reading guide, and checksums are downloadable beside the results. Those extracts let readers inspect the reported values, but the full source and run artifacts needed to reproduce the study are not public. The games and networks are both small by design, which is what makes several hundred training runs affordable, but it also means the conclusions are about small-network neuroevolution specifically, not neuroevolution at scale. The hyperparameter sweep is one-factor-at-a-time: it identifies which single knobs matter but, as the recurrence result shows, not how they interact, and the full interaction surface was not mapped.

A few modeling choices simplify the games themselves. Pac-Man's three ghosts use straight-line Manhattan targeting at half of Pac-Man's own speed, which keeps the maze winnable, rather than the arcade original's timing-based, personality-driven chase logic. 2048's illegal-move handling, a small penalty plus a cap on consecutive illegal moves before ending the episode, is one reasonable policy among several. The checkpoint serializer is built to round-trip the project's own files, not hardened against malformed input, so it is safe for personal runs and not a trusted import format for files from elsewhere.

Where it is heading

The repository is private while some early experimental history gets cleaned up before anyone else reads it. Opening the source is the plan once that pass is done, and a code link replaces this page's current no-repo state at that point.

The newer C++20 line of work, still in the same working tree, keeps moving on its own fifth game. Its stated next steps are a raw or combined pixel-observation mode (currently hand-built feature vectors only) and river and log lanes to complete the hazard set (currently road hazards only), both open work rather than finished.

Memorization ablation: fixed vs. random training worlds · 2026-07 · completed

Setup

Same population and hyperparameters, two training regimes per game. "Fixed world" reuses one world seed for the entire run, the setup a train/test split would normally guard against. "Random world" generates a fresh world every generation, the project's default everywhere else in this case study. Both regimes were then scored on a world neither had ever trained on, a genuinely unseen batch built from seeds the trainer never touched while selecting a champion.

Result

The gap lands exactly where the design predicts. 2048 shows it sharpest: fixed-world training has a reported score of 128 on its training world and 6 on an unseen one, while random-world training holds at 64 on training and 42 unseen, a much smaller drop. Snake and Pac-Man show the same shape. Snake reaches 31 apples on its own fixed training world but 10 on an unseen one, against 12 and 25 when trained random. Pac-Man reaches 40 and 15, against 66 and 41. Flappy shows the same advantage for random-world training: fixed-world training scores 6 on the training world and 2 unseen, while random-world training scores 54 on both. Snake's random-world unseen score (25) even edges past its own random-world training score (12), a reminder of how noisy a single-run comparison is at this scale.

What it changed

Generation-varying world seeds were already the trainer's default going into this ablation. The recorded comparison supports that default: all four games score higher on the unseen world after random-world training than after fixed-world training. This is evidence for varied training worlds in this ablation, not proof that held-out evaluation is unnecessary.

Champion selection: training fitness vs. a fresh selection batch · 2026-07 · completed

previously

The memorization ablation showed what happens when a population trains on a fixed world: all four games score lower on the unseen world than their random-world-trained counterparts in the recorded comparison. That supports training on varied worlds in this ablation. This experiment asks a narrower question about that same random-world scheme: once training worlds are already random, does it still matter how the champion gets picked at the end of each generation?

Setup

Fitness on any one generation's random worlds is noisy, since some generations simply serve up easier worlds than others. The trainer re-tests its top candidates and current champion at configured intervals on a fresh random batch, seeded from the run's master seed and generation number, and keeps the highest batch mean ("testbatch"). This comparison contrasts that scheme with selecting on raw training fitness ("fitness"), across three run seeds per game. The retained experiment script specifies 150 generations and then evaluates each saved champion on the same 30 seeds, 3000 through 3029, capped at 6,000 ticks per episode. The downloadable CSV retains the verifier's integer-truncated batch means; individual episode scores are not included.

Result

Testbatch selection has a higher three-seed mean in three games. Rounded means are Flappy 54 versus 51, Snake 27 versus 26, and 2048 38 versus 35. Individual runs are mixed: Snake seed 2 scores 25 versus 28, and 2048 seed 3 scores 33 versus 38, both favoring fitness selection. Pac-Man has identical paired scores of 33, 29, and 29 under both methods, a mean of 30.3. These modest average differences do not establish a reliable advantage from three seeds.

What it changed

Fresh-batch selection remains the trainer's default, and this comparison gives a modest reason to retain it. The useful design distinction is between the score that selects a champion and the held-out score used to assess that choice. Reusing the selection batch as final evidence would erase that distinction. More independent runs would be needed to determine whether the small mean differences persist.
