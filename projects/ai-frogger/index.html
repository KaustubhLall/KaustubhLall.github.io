A policy that survived without crossing

The learned Frogger policy stayed alive for an entire episode by moving left and right. It never moved toward the goal. That behavior earned a better average reward than random movement or a greedy policy that walked straight into traffic, so reward alone could have made the run look like progress. Completed crossings told a different story.

I built this project to put policies through the same seeded road and river hazards, then inspect what their scores mean. The first bounded genetic-training run produced a usable experiment and an unsuccessful crossing policy. The useful result is the explanation the saved evidence makes possible: which actions it took, how episodes ended, and which part of the reward deserves testing next.

Crossing is a timing problem

The goal is simple: cross traffic, ride moving logs across the river, and reach the far side. A legal move can still put the frog in danger after the hazards advance. A policy therefore needs information about what will happen next, rather than just the direction of the goal. The observation supplies local tiles, valid actions, and directional danger predictions for the next tick.

That distinction gives the baselines a purpose. Greedy moves upward whenever it can. The danger-aware policies check the next-tick hazard before advancing or sidestepping. The labels scripted and heuristic currently use equivalent action-selection logic, despite different decision text, so their matching results provide one reference behavior under two names.

One environment, several ways to inspect it

FroggerEnv owns the configuration, seeded state, and diagnostics. A step applies the selected movement, advances traffic and log riding, resolves terminal conditions, and records a reward breakdown. Headless evaluation, genetic training, benchmarking, and the optional raylib viewer all use that core. A policy change can therefore be compared without also changing the game underneath it.

The agent interface separates observation from action selection, and saved weights can be loaded into the evaluator after training ends. Metrics exports retain episode rewards, lengths, terminal counts, and action distributions. The viewer reads the same state and decision information, but graphics are optional. Experiments and tests can run without a window, while replay and rendering still have their own verification requirements.

What the genetic algorithm is selecting

The learned policy is a linear scoring function. It flattens the observation, scores each action with a weight array, and chooses the highest-scoring valid action. There are no hidden layers or recurrent memory. The trainer evaluates candidate arrays over episodes, ranks their mean rewards, retains elites, and fills the population with mutated descendants. The best weights seen are saved for later evaluation.

This makes fitness the decisive interface between game design and learning. The trainer has no separate understanding of crossing, progress, or an unhelpful sidestep unless those distinctions affect its reward. It also has a comparison weakness: training maps depend on population index. Candidates are ranked after playing different maps, so their fitness combines policy behavior with the difficulty of the maps assigned to them. The run cannot isolate genetic selection quality from that difference.

First, make the measurement trustworthy

Before relying on an optimized test run, I repaired the assertion contract. Release builds define NDEBUG, and some tests had put persistence operations inside C assertions. Those builds could skip both the checks and the operations the tests appeared to exercise. The test targets now keep assertions active and have a compile-time guard against disabling them. The recorded local Release and Debug runs each passed all 12 tests.

The result export had a second trap. A standalone timeout counter increments twice for runner timeouts. The terminal-reason export counts episodes correctly, so the comparison uses those counts and checks that they reconcile with the episode total. These repairs establish what was measured. They do not make the policy more capable.

A small run with a separate test set

The declared training plan used 12 candidates for 10 generations, 8 episodes per candidate, 4 elites, and a mutation probability of 0.05. Episodes used the default crossing layout and a 500-step limit. This was a bounded run, with no tuning permitted against its later held-out result.

After training, the saved policy and all four baseline labels played the same 200 seeds, disjoint from the declared training range. The automatic evaluation printed by the trainer overlaps training seeds and is excluded here. The downloadable plan, configuration, weights, raw summaries, and per-episode exports identify the exact comparison. Holding maps and episode limits fixed at evaluation makes the behavioral differences readable even though candidate fairness during training remains unresolved.

What the reward concealed

The danger-aware labels each completed 159 of 200 crossings. Random and greedy completed none, and neither did the learned policy. Every learned-policy episode reached the 500-step limit. Across its 100,000 actions, the policy chose only left and right, with no upward moves, downward moves, or waits.

Its average reward was -0.1000, compared with -0.8318 for random, -0.6821 for greedy, and 5.3792 for the danger-aware behavior. It had learned a better-scoring way to fail than the first two baselines. The goal counts and action distribution prevent that narrow improvement from becoming a claim that it learned Frogger. This result concerns one saved policy on one fixed evaluation set, and does not establish a limit of linear policies or genetic algorithms.

The trained policy did not learn to cross

The neuro policy won 0/200 episodes and timed out in all 200. Its recorded actions were 49,400 left and 50,600 right, with no forward moves. Scripted and heuristic policies are behaviorally equivalent here, so their matching results are not evidence from two independent algorithms.

The next hypothesis comes from the actions

The reward code penalizes WAIT for stalling, but valid horizontal movement does not receive that penalty merely for making no progress. A timeout has a separate terminal penalty. This is consistent with the observed policy: move sideways, avoid an early death, and accept the timeout. The code provides a plausible incentive for the behavior, and the action counts show that the behavior occurred.

That is still a hypothesis about why training selected this policy. No reward ablation isolated the effect, and candidate-specific maps remain a second influence. A useful follow-up would first make candidate comparisons fair, then test a reward intervention under a declared protocol. Increasing the training budget alone would leave both questions unresolved.

What is inspectable now

The local environment, trainer, saved-policy loading, and evaluation exports work, and the evidence package preserves the failed policy alongside its baselines. A separate heuristic replay reaches the goal in 16 actions and has a deterministic native verification. It illustrates successful baseline behavior, not a learned-policy success, and requires native tooling rather than a browser demo.

The viewer builds, but rendered playback and controls were not accepted in the September check. Configuration loading also supports a limited layout rather than a general validated JSON contract. Those are tooling boundaries. The research boundary is different: the existing held-out set has now informed the analysis, so the next training claim needs a new evaluation plan. The immediate question is whether a controlled reward change produces crossing rather than another higher-scoring failure.
