    summary

    This was my first sustained research problem: use computation to understand why two kidney transporters that looked broadly redundant behaved differently in living systems. I learned to move between biology and code—asking what a molecular feature meant, then testing whether it survived exhaustive search, resampling, and an external drug set.

    • JBC 2020 paper↗

    key responsibilities

    • ●Built the Python machine-learning implementation behind the study: data preparation and visualization, Random Forest and Decision Tree baselines, exhaustive feature-combination search, and NEAT-based feature selection and engineering. The code tested all 480,700 seven-feature combinations available from a 25-feature pool and repeatedly resampled cross-validation to avoid promoting a lucky split.
    • ●Helped reduce more than 60 structural and physicochemical descriptors to seven interpretable properties that classified unique OAT1 versus OAT3 metabolites at roughly 75–80% accuracy. The biology became legible: OAT3 tended toward larger, more complex molecules with more rings and chiral centers, while OAT1 favored smaller structures with different surface-area characteristics.
    • ●Applied the metabolite-derived model to a small, independent set of drugs with strong transporter-affinity differences; at least 75% were classified correctly. We treated that as evidence of feasibility, not a production predictor—the drug set contained only 33 compounds and the paper explicitly discusses the risk of overdetermination.
    • ●The outcome was a 2020 Journal of Biological Chemistry paper and an open implementation, but the more important result was conceptual: OAT1 and OAT3 were not simply interchangeable clearance routes, and interpretable ML could help connect knockout metabolomics to transporter physiology and future drug–metabolite questions.
